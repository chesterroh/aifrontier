<span class="paragraph-timestamp" data-ts="1:35">1:35</span> Beyond coding, there are many uses, like summarizing documents, but companies and most people are still far behind, so we're at a very early stage. When steam engines arrived, work people had done by hand at home was brought into factories and made more efficient. Early on, one large steam engine would run, turning a main shaft through a system of pipes. Numerous belts were connected to that shaft to distribute power to the factory's upper and lower floors. But when electric motors first arrived, factories kept that setup and simply replaced the steam engine with one large electric motor. Factory owners asked whether electricity would make things run faster and get the work done sooner. It turned out it didn't.

<span class="paragraph-timestamp" data-ts="3:30">3:30</span> So when did factory productivity really increase? When electric motors became small enough to be installed in different factory machines, each for its own purpose, and factories began redesigning their layouts completely. That's when productivity rose. Likewise, we're still using systems for knowledge work that we've built over nearly a century. Unless the entire workflow changes, the transformation won't happen. That's the argument people are making.

## Why Accounting Firms Trust Only SAP Data    *31:02*
